## Executive conclusion

Recommended Year-1 averages:
Tier A £35k; Tier B and Tier C £30k
The evidence does not support a mechanical Tier A > Tier B > Tier C transaction-size
ladder. Document maturity and transaction value are correlated only indirectly through
customer and sector mix. The recommended base case gives Tier A a modest £5k
premium because larger firms adopt eBL more frequently, while holding Tier B and Tier C
equal because paper documentation remains common in high-value trade and Tier C
pricing makes genuinely small deals unattractive.

### Why the growth rates differ
World merchandise trade volume is forecast to grow only 1.9% in 2026 and 2.6% in 2027, while the IMF
expected UAE inflation around 2% over the medium term. Those facts support a low-single-digit nominal
baseline, not 35% annual average-deal growth. [S21] [S22]

The recommended rates therefore use an explicit planning decomposition: roughly 3% for nominal trade-
value growth, plus 7% for Tier A, 5% for Tier B and 2% for Tier C from repeat-customer trust, larger
customer acquisition and mix progression. These uplifts are assumptions, not observed causal
estimates. Tier C grows slowest because successful customers and document flows should migrate toward
B or A rather than making the manual tier indefinitely larger.

## 2. Evidence method and limitations

Statistical limitation. No public source found provides a transaction-level dataset linking deal
value, SME status, corridor, sector, shipment mode and Tier A/B/C verification method. A true tier-
level mean, median or interquartile range cannot be calculated from public data. Aggregate value-
per-document ratios are presented as proxies; planning ranges are not relabelled as empirical
quartiles.

### Three approaches were reconciled
1. Top-down. Official trade values were divided only by named denominators such as customs
declarations, Shipping Bills, Bills of Entry or certificates of origin. The denominator and its limitations
are stated in every row.
2. Bottom-up. WITS/UN Comtrade values and physical quantities were used to derive unit values for
target-sector goods. Explicit shipment-quantity assumptions then produce illustrative transaction
ranges.
3. Comparable product. Blockmediary’s fee schedule was compared with current general escrow
prices and UAE documentary-credit fee schedules. Product differences are retained: a bank LC
includes a bank undertaking, while Blockmediary is prefunded escrow and does not lend.

### Interpretation
• Broad Dubai activity: ~£16k. Dubai non-oil trade is inferred from DIEZ’s AED282bn representing
13.5% of Dubai non-oil trade, then divided by more than 26.5m declarations. This is too broad to use
directly because the denominator includes many small and administrative customs events. [S2] [S3]
• Maritime/wholesale activity: ~£39k. Hamriyah and Deira ports processed 62,454 declarations
worth AED12.133bn in the first nine months of 2023. This is closer to Blockmediary’s intended
goods-trade profile but excludes Jebel Ali and covers only two ports. [S4]
• India export forms: ~£30k-£35k. India’s 9.2m annual Shipping Bills combined with FY2024-25
export values imply about £30k per non-petroleum Shipping Bill and £35k across all merchandise
exports. This is one of the strongest anchors for UAE-India SME exports, though a Shipping Bill is not
necessarily one invoice. [S7] [S8]
• Exporter/chamber upper anchor: ~£79k-£84k. Dubai Chamber export/re-export value divided
by certificates of origin is biased toward established exporters and larger firms. The implied ratio
increased about 6.9% from 2024 to 2025—well below 35%, but one year is not a forecast. [S5] [S6]

### Shipment mode and arrangement
• LCL versus FCL. Maersk describes LCL as suitable for loads below 15 CBM or 2-3 pallets. CMA CGM
lists a 20ft dry container at 33.2m³ and 28.25t payload, and a 40ft unit at 67.8m³ with payload up to
28.78t. These physical measures influence quantity, but not value density. [S15] [S16]
• Forwarder versus direct carrier. Forwarders consolidate LCL but also arrange FCL. There is no
credible public basis for applying a universal forwarder discount or direct-carrier premium to deal
value.
• SME size. Smaller SMEs often order less, but repeat importers may consolidate purchases, and an
SME can import a single high-value machine. Turnover is not a usable substitute for invoice value.
• Sector. The planning examples range from roughly £17k-£22k for dense low-value goods to £55k+
for one generator and up to £82k for an illustrative textiles load. The early customer and sector mix
therefore matters more than the tier label.

Reconciliation. The midpoint of the six illustrative bottom-up ranges is roughly in the low-£40k
area, but this is not a sample mean: the goods were deliberately selected to span the target
sectors. It supports a conservative £30k-£35k launch base rather than proving one.

## 5. Document maturity and transaction size

### No credible public evidence proves eBL transactions are systematically larger
DCSA reported actual eBL adoption around 4% in 2024, up from 2.1% in 2022, and later described around
11% of bills as electronic in 2025. The FIT Alliance’s respondent survey found 49.2% using eBL in some
capacity, 50% in the Middle East and 29.4% among freight forwarders. The difference between actual bill
share and respondent usage illustrates measurement and selection effects. [S17] [S18] [S19]

The survey does provide one directional link: firms with more than 250 employees adopt eBL more
frequently. However, it explicitly says industry type is the strongest factor. The first standards-based
interoperable eBL transaction was completed only in May 2025, and DCSA identifies platform
fragmentation as a prior constraint. [S18] [S20]

premium is therefore deliberately small, not structural.

### What the Year-1 averages represent
The recommended bases assume a small, selected launch cohort: repeat mid-market SME importers/
exporters or a forwarder-led cluster, with transactions screened for documentary suitability and
willingness to prefund. They do not represent all customs declarations or all SMEs. Selection raises the
expected average above the broad £16k declaration proxy, but limited trust and an early operating cap
justify keeping the bases below exporter-oriented £79k-£84k proxies.

### Minimum deal size and internal cost
At this preliminary stage, internal variable cost is not yet evidenced by operating data. The break-even rule is:

If variable cost ≤ minimum escrow fee + document fee, any accepted deal covers
variable cost.
Otherwise, minimum deal value = (variable cost − document fee) / escrow rate.

At the recommended Year-1 bases, the total fee revenue available to cover payment, compliance,
verification and support costs is £252.50 for Tier A, £360 for Tier B and £900 for Tier C. A 50%
contribution-margin target would cap variable costs at £126.25, £180 and £450 respectively. These are
cost ceilings, not estimates of actual cost.

### £50,000 human-review threshold
The prior baseline average crosses the cap in Year 4. Under the base recommendation, Tier A crosses in
Year 5; Tier B and Tier C do not cross by Year 5. Under high sensitivities, Tier A begins at £50k in Year 1,

based on the value distribution rather than only the average.

## 10. Final decision statement

### Recommended numbers

Tier           Year-1 average           USD comparison       Annual growth    Year-5 average    Confidence

Tier A                  £35,000                    $47,000            10%             £51,000   Medium-low

Tier B                  £30,000                    $41,000             8%             £41,000   Medium

Tier C                  £30,000                    $41,000             5%             £36,500   Medium-low

### Three strongest supporting facts
1. India’s official export values and annual Shipping Bill count imply about £30k per non-petroleum
export document and £35k per all-merchandise export document—close to the proposed launch
bases. [S7] [S8]
2. A targeted Dubai maritime-port ratio implies roughly £39k per customs declaration, while the much
broader all-Dubai ratio is about £16k and chamber certificate proxies are about £79k-£84k. The
proposed £30k-£35k values sit conservatively within that hierarchy. [S2] [S3] [S4] [S5] [S6]
3. eBL evidence shows larger firms adopt more often but industry type is the stronger determinant, so
only a modest Tier A premium is justified and no strict tier-size ordering is defensible. [S18]

### Three greatest uncertainties
1. The true launch-cohort median, quartiles and right tail by sector, corridor and tier.
2. The extent to which repeat customers entrust larger transactions to Blockmediary, separate from
nominal trade growth.
3. The actual time and cost of manual/document exception review, especially above £50k.

Overall confidence: medium-low. The base case is credible for academic and investor review because it is
conservative and triangulated. Confidence would increase after transaction-level forwarder or pilot data
are obtained.
